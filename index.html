To clean the pond, we first have to understand it

Our big goal is to clean the pond at Diablo Valley College without hurting its ecosystem. Many animals and microorganisms live there, and we don’t want to kill them. If we learn how the ecosystem works, we can choose how, and with which chemicals, to make the pond cleaner.

The project is also a way to gain research experience. In research you don’t know what you’ll find. When we grew bacteria, we couldn’t know in advance what would show up. Using a microscope and culturing bacteria are new for many of us, and learning them is part of the point.

Questions we’re asking

  • Does dirtier water hold more bacteria?
  • Do decaying leaves release phosphate that feeds algae and bacteria?
  • Do dead algae, and the bacteria that break them down, use up the oxygen?
  • Are the bacteria we find aerobic or anaerobic?
  • Would the chemicals used to “clean” water make bacteria grow, and how fast?

Next: Growing bacteria

How we grow and sort pond bacteria

Bacteria are small and fast, and even methyl cellulose didn’t slow them enough to study under the microscope. So we grow them on plates, where each colony is many identical cells.

  1. Collect pond water. Dilute it (10⁻¹, 10⁻², 10⁻³) if the plates would be too crowded.
  2. Plate it on nutrient agar and on blood agar.
  3. Incubate at 30 °C and at room temperature, and check every day.
  4. Record each colony: color, shape, elevation, margin, size, and growth rate.
  5. Isolate colonies with a four-quadrant streak, so each plate holds one kind of bacteria.
  6. Gram stain the isolates to sort them into two groups.

Aseptic technique at BSL-1

Our work follows BSL-1 practice, the level for low-risk bacteria not known to cause human disease. The goal is to keep air, hands, the bench, and other bacteria out of our cultures.

  • Wear gloves and tie your hair back.
  • Wipe the bench with disinfectant.
  • Label plates on the bottom.
  • Open the lid only a little, like a clamshell, and never talk over an open plate.
  • Flame a metal loop until red hot, then let it cool for 10 to 15 seconds without waving it. A hot loop kills your sample. Disposable plastic loops need no flame.
  • Four-quadrant streak: streak the first area heavily, flame the loop, drag into the second quadrant, flame, drag into the third, flame, drag into the fourth. Isolated colonies should appear in the last one.

Reading blood agar plates

Blood agar shows three patterns. Alpha hemolysis turns the plate greenish. Beta hemolysis leaves clear zones where blood cells were completely broken down. Gamma hemolysis changes nothing.

Growth on blood agar is a flag, not a verdict. Ordinary water and soil bacteria such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Aeromonas can grow there too. We need a Gram stain and DNA identification before calling anything harmful.
Gram stain: sorting bacteria by color
  • Crystal violet for 1 minute stains every cell purple. Rinse gently.
  • Gram’s iodine for 1 minute locks the dye in. Rinse gently.
  • Decolorizer (alcohol or acetone) for 1 to 5 seconds, until the runoff is clear. This step matters most. Rinse right away.
  • Safranin for 30 to 60 seconds stains the decolorized cells pink. Rinse gently.
  • Blot dry (don’t wipe) and view at 100× with oil. Gram-positive cells stay purple. Gram-negative cells turn pink.

Tips: keep the smear thin, and use young cultures (18 to 24 hours). Over-decolorizing makes Gram-positive cells look pink.

From plate to DNA to gel

You can’t go straight from a plate to a gel. The DNA is trapped inside the bacteria, so nothing would show up. We have to open the cells and isolate the DNA first.

Grows the organisms. You see colonies.

Extraction

Lifts out their blueprint: lyse the cells, remove proteins, precipitate the DNA.

Gel

Sorts the DNA pieces by size. You see bands.

How agarose gel electrophoresis works

DNA is negatively charged, so an electric current pulls it through a gel made from seaweed agarose toward the positive electrode. The gel acts like a sieve: small pieces slip through the pores faster and travel farther. A fluorescent dye makes the bands glow under UV or blue light. A ladder of known sizes, from 100 to 1,000 base pairs, lets us estimate how long our fragments are.

Labs use gels to check DNA quality, verify PCR, and map restriction sites. We still need to confirm which of these tools our lab has.

Our proposal: name the bacteria in the pond

Knowing which bacteria live in the pond tells us more about how it works.

  • Nutrient cycling: some bacteria break down dead organisms and recycle carbon and nitrogen.
  • Ecosystem health: the bacterial community hints at pollution, nutrient levels, and changing water conditions.
  • Interactions: bacteria can support algae and plants, compete with other microbes, or cause disease.
  • Biofilms: some bacteria form communities on rocks, plants, and other surfaces.
  • Change over time: comparing samples shows how the community shifts with temperature, rainfall, or pollution.

Identification workflow

  1. Collect and culture pond samples in our supervised school lab.
  2. Make pure cultures, so each isolate holds one kind of bacteria.
  3. Extract DNA: lysis opens the cells, purification separates the DNA.
  4. Check DNA quality and quantity against the receiving lab’s requirements.
  5. Sequence the 16S rRNA gene, a common bacterial ID marker. PCR is usually part of this step, so we’ll confirm whether our school or the sequencing lab does it.
  6. Compare sequences with reference databases to find the closest matches.

Studies that go with it

Bacterial diversity map

Plate diluted pond water and record each colony’s color, shape, elevation, size, and growth rate.

Antibiotic resistance

Spread pond bacteria on agar, add antibiotic disks (ampicillin, tetracycline), and measure each zone of inhibition.

Biofilm formation

Grow bacteria in tubes with a plastic or glass surface, stain with crystal violet, and measure biofilm density.

What the results can and can’t tell us

Our results describe the bacteria that grow under our culture conditions, not the whole pond community. To study the community, we’d extract DNA straight from pond samples and use 16S amplicon sequencing. Closely related species can’t always be told apart by 16S alone, and identifying a bacterium doesn’t prove what it does. Showing nutrient cycling or effects on water quality takes extra measurements.

Biosafety: we work at BSL-1 in a supervised school lab. Some pond bacteria may need BSL-2 precautions, so we review that with our supervisor before any new organism work.
